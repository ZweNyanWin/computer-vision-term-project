How many photographs make a frog?

This is a kob mai — a Thai wooden frog, carved from a single block of hardwood, hollowed into a resonator and played by scraping a beater down its ridged back. It is a craft object and a musical instrument at once. Drag it to turn it around. Then change how many photographs the computer was allowed to see, and watch what survives.

What each one costs, and what it cannot do

One photograph → a relief

Three seconds of computing. A neural network guesses depth from shading alone, so you get the side that faced the camera and nothing behind it. Turn it and the frog thins to a sliver, then vanishes. That is not a bug: the information was never in the photograph.

45 photographs → a closed surface

Eighty seconds of photogrammetry. Because every side was photographed, the model closes: about half its 50,000 triangles face you from any angle, which is what "closed surface" means. The underside is still missing — the frog sat on a table.

88 photographs → Gaussian Splatting

Twenty-three minutes on a laptop GPU. 140,018 translucent blobs, fitted so that re-photographing them near the captured camera path reproduces the original. It keeps the grain the mesh loses — but it is a cloud, not a surface, so you cannot 3D-print it. For this turntable, 58,981 connected frog blobs are isolated from the captured room and shown on black.

Does it actually work? We hid twelve photographs and checked.

Twelve of the 88 photographs were locked away before Gaussian fitting. Their pixels never entered the training loss. Afterwards we placed a virtual camera at exactly the spot each hidden photograph was taken from, rendered what the model predicted, and compared it against the photograph whose pixels were withheld.

Said precisely: this is held-out photometric evaluation over a shared reconstruction. No hidden pixel was ever trained on — but the camera positions were solved using all 88 photographs together, so 36% of the starting points were informed by the hidden views. That is the standard way this is measured, and it is worth saying out loud rather than claiming the hidden photographs played no part at all.

How these views were rendered

The one- and 45-photograph meshes use the renderer written for this project: perspective projection x = K[R|t]X, back-face culling, far-to-near ordering, per-triangle texture mapping and Lambertian shading in NumPy and OpenCV. The 88-photograph version uses MLX3D's Gaussian rasteriser; project code recovers and drives its camera orbit, removes the support plane and disconnected room splats for this presentation view, and composites the frog on black. The evaluated 140,018-Gaussian checkpoint itself is unchanged. Both paths wrote these frames in advance, so playing the station needs no internet and no GPU.
